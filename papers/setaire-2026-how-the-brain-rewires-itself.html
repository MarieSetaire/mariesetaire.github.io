How the Brain Rewires Itself When Ears Stop Working

Marie Setaire

25 June 2026.

Download PDF

Summary

This article doesn’t require any knowledge about the brain and hearing. I want this knowledge to be accessible to anyone! Imagine your brain is like a big house with lots of rooms. Each room has a job. One special room is in charge of hearing — it’s called the auditory cortex. But here’s something amazing: if that “hearing room” stops getting sounds, the brain doesn’t just leave it empty. It gives the room a new job, usually helping with seeing instead (Lee et al., 2001; Sadato et al., 2004). Scientists call this “cross-modal reorganization,” which is just a fancy way of saying “the brain swapping one job for another.”

For a long time, scientists thought this swap was a totally good thing — the brain being clever and not wasting space. But it turns out the story is more complicated, and that’s what this article is about: Is the swap helpful, or does it cause problems?

The brain swapping the “hearing room” into a “seeing room” isn’t simply good or bad — it depends on when it starts, how long it lasts, and who it happens to. Catching it early, while the brain is still bendy and willing to change back, gives each person the best chance to hear the world clearly again.
